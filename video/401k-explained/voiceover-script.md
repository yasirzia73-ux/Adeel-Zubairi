## Script (one line per caption)

### Hook
- Your job might be offering you free money every single year.
- And millions of people never claim it.
- It's called the 401(k) match. Let's break it down in plain English.

### What is a 401(k)
- Jake just started a new job. In his welcome packet, there was a form he almost threw away.
- A 401(k) enrollment form.
- A 401(k) is a retirement account you get through your job.
- You pick a percentage of your paycheck, and it goes in automatically, before you ever see it.
- That money gets invested, usually in funds that own hundreds of companies, so it can grow for decades.
- And the name? It's just the section of the tax code that created it.

### The match
- Now, the part most people miss: the employer match.
- Many companies add money to your 401(k) when you contribute.
- A common deal: they add 50 cents for every dollar you put in, up to 6% of your pay.
- Jake earns $60,000 a year. If he puts in 6%, that's $3,600 a year.
- His company adds another $1,800. For free.
- That's an instant 50% return, before the market does anything at all.
- But if Jake puts in nothing, his company adds nothing. The match just disappears.

### The cost
- So what does skipping the match really cost?
- $1,800 a year is $150 a month.
- Invested from age 25 to 65 at a 7% average return, the match alone could grow to about $394,000.
- That's what people leave on the table by skipping one form.

### Taxes
- There's also a tax bonus.
- With a traditional 401(k), your contributions come out before income tax.
- Many people earning $60,000 are in the 12% federal bracket, so Jake's $3,600 cuts his tax bill by about $432 this year.
- He pays the tax later, when he takes the money out in retirement.
- Many plans also offer a Roth 401(k): you pay the tax now, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
- Either way, the match still counts. The real mistake is choosing neither.

### 4 rules
- Four rules to know.
- One: in 2026, you can put in up to $24,500 of your own money. More if you're 50 or older.
- Two: it's meant for retirement. Take money out before age 59 and a half, and you'll usually pay a 10% penalty plus income tax.
- Three: your own contributions are always yours, but the match may vest over a few years. Leave early, and you could lose part of it.
- Four: if you change jobs, your 401(k) comes with you. Roll it into your new plan or an IRA. Don't cash it out.

### How to start
- Here's how to start this week.
- Step one: ask HR two questions. What is the match, and when does it vest?
- Step two: contribute at least enough to get the full match.
- Step three: pick a low-cost fund. A target-date fund for the year you plan to retire is a simple default.
- Step four: turn on automatic 1% yearly increases, if your plan offers them.
- And if you were enrolled automatically, often at just 3%, check that it's enough to get the full match.

### End
