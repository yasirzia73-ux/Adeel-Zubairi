## (no heading: start straight in)

October twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-six. Eight oh five P.M., universal time.  
Money starts quietly leaving the world's biggest crypto exchange.  
Not a few dollars. Billions.  
By the time it stops, about six point three billion dollars is gone.  
No masks. No getaway car. No alarms going off. Just code.  
It's the largest crypto theft in history. More than four times the previous record.  
And the FBI says the thieves weren't a lone hacker in a basement. They were working for a country.  
It all started with one laptop.  
Here's exactly how it happened, and what it means for your money.  

## JAKE'S MORNING

Meet Jake. He's fictional, but his problem was very real. He keeps about three thousand dollars in crypto on Binance.  
On October thirtieth, he opens the app and sees a message he's never seen before:  
Withdrawals temporarily suspended.  
His money is still on the screen. He just can't move it.  
Millions of users around the world saw the same thing. So what was going on?  

## HOT WALLETS vs COLD WALLETS

First, one quick lesson that explains everything: hot wallets and cold wallets.  
An exchange like Binance holds a huge amount of its customers' crypto. Reports put its total assets at around one hundred seventy-one billion dollars.  
Most of it sits in cold wallets. Think of a bank vault: offline, locked away, and slow to open.  
But people want to withdraw instantly, so some of the money sits in hot wallets.  
A hot wallet is like the cash register at the front of a store. Online, connected, always ready to pay out.  
Convenient. But if someone takes control of the register, they can empty it.  

## THE HEIST

So how did the thieves get to the register?  
According to Binance and early security reports, it started with a single engineer's laptop.  
Attackers compromised that computer and used it to reach the system that approves hot wallet transactions.  
Binance says no private keys were stolen. The attackers didn't need them.  
Instead, they got the hot wallets to sign a kind of digital permission slip, using a newer Ethereum feature called E-I-P seventy-seven-oh-two.  
Think of it like getting the cashier to sign a note that says: from now on, my friend can open this register.  
Security analysts stress the feature itself wasn't broken. The attackers abused the signing process.  
Once the permission slips were signed, the money started moving. Wallet after wallet, chain after chain.  
Reports say seventeen hot wallets across nine different blockchains were drained.  
Billions of dollars, moving in a matter of hours, while most of the world was asleep.  
Binance paused withdrawals. That's the message Jake saw.  

## WHO DID IT

Two days later, the FBI named the group behind it: TraderTraitor.  
It's a North Korean state-backed hacking team, also known as the Lazarus Group.  
And they have a track record.  
In past warnings, the FBI described one of their favorite tricks: fake job offers sent to employees at crypto companies, with malware hidden inside.  
One click on the wrong file, and they are in.  
In twenty twenty-two, they took about six hundred twenty-five million dollars from the Ronin network, behind the game Axie Infinity.  
In twenty twenty-five, they stole about one point five billion dollars from the exchange Bybit.  
Now, roughly six point three billion dollars. Each heist bigger than the last.  
United Nations experts have reported that North Korea uses stolen crypto to help fund its weapons programs.  
So this isn't just a hack. It's one of the ways a sanctioned country raises money.  

## THE CHASE

But here's where it gets interesting. Stealing crypto is easy compared to spending it.  
Every blockchain transaction is public. Investigators can watch the stolen coins move in real time.  
And companies that issue stablecoins, like Tether and Circle, can freeze their tokens on the spot.  
Within days, Tether, Circle, Ripple, Arbitrum and Optimism had frozen roughly two point eight five billion dollars of the stolen assets.  
So the hackers race to swap what is left into Ethereum, then try to wash it through dee-fye apps and instant swap services.  
It becomes a chase. And it can go on for months.  
Some of that money will probably never be recovered.  

## BINANCE'S RESPONSE

Meanwhile, Binance had to answer one question: is customer money safe?  
Co-CEO Richard Teng told users: your assets are safe. Binance would cover the losses with company funds.  
Binance has an emergency fund for exactly this, called "safe-you", set up in twenty eighteen with one billion dollars. This time it needed far more, so the company paid the rest.  
By November first, Binance said it had replaced about ninety-five percent of the stolen assets with its own money.  
Withdrawals reopened in phases, starting with fifteen networks, including Bitcoin, Ethereum and Solana.  
Then people rushed for the exits. About four point seven billion dollars flowed out in the first days, roughly three percent of Binance's assets.  
Bitcoin dropped as much as five percent, briefly falling below seventy-six thousand dollars.  
And Jake? He got his three thousand dollars back. This time.  

## THE REAL LESSON

And that's the part worth thinking about.  
Jake was fine because Binance happened to have billions of dollars to cover the hole.  
Not every exchange can do that.  
When F-T-X collapsed in twenty twenty-two, customers waited more than two years to get money back. That wasn't a hack, but the lesson is the same:  
When your crypto sits on an exchange, you don't fully control it. You're trusting the company.  
Crypto has a saying for this: not your keys, not your coins.  

## 5 WAYS TO PROTECT YOUR CRYPTO

So here's how to protect yourself.  
One: don't keep everything on one exchange. Spread out the risk.  
Two: for long-term holdings, consider a hardware wallet, where you hold the keys yourself. Just guard your recovery phrase carefully.  
Three: watch out for scams after a big hack. Fake refund emails and fake support calls surge. A real exchange will never ask for your password or recovery phrase.  
Four: use an authenticator app or a passkey for two-factor login, not text messages.  
Five: only put in money you can afford to lose. Crypto can swing hard, hack or no hack.

The story isn't over. Investigators are still tracking the money, and we'll keep you updated.
