Before we get to your wallet, you need to understand why the Fed would raise rates when everyone hates higher rates. Because once you understand the why, you can predict what happens next.

The Fed has two jobs. Keep people employed, and keep prices stable. Right now, job number one looks fine. Job number two does not.

In September, prices were up three point seven percent from a year earlier. The Fed's target is two percent. And a big driver was gas. Energy costs jumped, and when gas goes up, it leaks into everything: shipping, groceries, airline tickets.

Think of interest rates like the brakes on a car. When the economy is speeding and prices are running hot, the Fed taps the brakes. Borrowing gets more expensive, so people and businesses borrow and spend a little less, and prices slowly cool down.

The problem is that the brakes don't work instantly. It can take a year or more for a rate hike to fully hit the economy. So the Fed is basically driving while looking a year down the road, and that's exactly why their decisions feel confusing in the moment.

And this Fed chair has made it clear he'd rather brake too early than too late. Even with pressure from the White House, which has wanted lower rates, Warsh has said the economy is strong enough to absorb a few increases.

To guess what happens next, it helps to look at the last time the Fed did this, because it wasn't that long ago.

Here's what that did to regular people.

Mortgage rates more than doubled. They went from around three percent to nearly eight percent by the fall of twenty twenty-three. Millions of people who had planned to buy a home suddenly couldn't afford the same house anymore.

Credit card rates hit record highs. Car loans got more expensive. And the stock market had a rough year. In twenty twenty-two, the S and P five hundred fell about twenty-five percent from its peak.

But here's the part almost nobody talks about. Savers, for the first time in more than a decade, actually got paid. High-yield savings accounts went from paying almost nothing to paying four or five percent.

And the stock market? It didn't stay down. By twenty twenty-four, it was hitting new all-time highs. People who panicked and sold in twenty twenty-two locked in their losses. People who kept investing every month bought stocks on sale.

So the lesson from last time is simple. Rate hikes hurt borrowers right away. They reward savers right away. And for long-term investors, they're usually a storm you sit through, not a reason to abandon ship.

The difference this time is that we're not starting from zero. We're starting from almost four percent. So every hike lands on top of rates that are already high, and that's why this one matters.

Move one: attack your credit card debt. If you carry a balance, that's your biggest risk. Pay down the card with the highest rate first. And if you have good credit, look at a zero percent balance transfer card to pause the interest while you pay it off.
