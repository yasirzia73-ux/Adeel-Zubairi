# Voiceover script: "The Iran War Is Costing Your Family $2,000"

## HOOK

Since February, the average American household has quietly lost about two thousand dollars.  
Not to a scam. Not to a bad investment.  
To a war happening more than six thousand miles away.  
That's the estimate from Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics.  
And at current prices, he says the bill keeps growing by about fifty dollars every week.  
So where is that money actually going? And how do you keep more of it? Let's follow the money.  

## WHY (read aloud: "Where it starts.")

It starts with a narrow strip of water called the Strait of Hormuz.  
About a fifth of the world's oil normally passes through it on tankers.  
On February twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-six, the United States and Israel began air strikes on Iran.  
Two days later, Iran's Revolutionary Guard declared the strait closed.  
Oil traders panicked. Brent crude jumped from about seventy-two dollars a barrel to nearly one hundred twenty dollars at its peak.  
Even now, in early November, oil is still around one hundred dollars a barrel. And that number touches almost everything you buy.  

## PUMP (read aloud: "Maya's wallet.")

Meet Maya. She has never been anywhere near the Middle East. But she feels this war every single week.  
Before the war, gas averaged about two ninety-nine a gallon.  
By mid-October, it was four thirty-three. That is forty-five percent higher.  
Maya fills a fifteen-gallon tank once a week. That is about twenty dollars more, every week.  
Over a year, that is more than a thousand dollars. Just for driving to work.  

## RIPPLE (read aloud: "The ripple effect.")

And it doesn't stop at the pump.  
Diesel jumped even more, up sixty-eight percent, to six thirty-three a gallon.  
Diesel moves the trucks that carry almost everything to your local store. So shipping costs climb, and stores pass them on.  
Groceries. Packages. Even the plastic in everyday products.  
And with winter coming, home heating bills are rising too.  
Zandi estimates energy costs alone have taken about eleven hundred dollars from the typical family. That's the biggest slice.  

## RATES (read aloud: "The hidden cost.")

But the second slice is sneakier. It hides inside your interest rates.  
When energy gets expensive, inflation rises. In September, inflation hit three point seven percent, while wages grew only about three percent.  
To fight inflation, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September, and some officials want to go higher.  
In late February, a thirty-year mortgage averaged under six percent. By late October, it was above seven percent.  
On a three hundred fifty thousand dollars home loan, that's about two hundred ninety-one dollars more every month. Around thirty-five hundred dollars a year.  
Credit cards and car loans get more expensive too. Zandi puts this slice at about five hundred forty dollars per household.  

## BILL (read aloud: "The full bill.")

Here's the full bill, as Moody's estimates it.  
About eleven hundred dollars in energy.  
About five hundred forty dollars in higher interest rates.  
And about three hundred sixty dollars as each household's share of the cost of military operations.  
Total: roughly two thousand dollars per household. About two hundred sixty-five billion dollars across the whole country.  

## CAVEAT (read aloud: "A reality check.")

Now, a quick reality check.  
This is an economist's estimate, not a bill that lands in your mailbox. Your number could be much higher or much lower.  
If you drive a lot or carry credit card debt, you're probably paying more. If you rent and work from home, maybe less.  

## NEXT (read aloud: "What happens next?")

So what happens next? Nobody knows for sure, but there are three main paths.  
Path one: a diplomatic deal. In early November, oil prices dipped on exactly that hope. Gas could slowly come down.  
But here's the catch. Economists have a name for how gas prices move: rockets and feathers. They shoot up fast, and float down slowly.  
Path two: a long stalemate. Prices stay high, and that fifty dollars a week keeps adding up.  
Path three: escalation. If shipping through the strait is disrupted again, oil could spike past its old peak.  
You can't pick the path. But you can get your budget ready for all three.  

## PROTECT (read aloud: "Five ways to keep more of your money.")

So here's how to keep more of your money, no matter what happens next.  
One: cut your gas bill. Compare prices with an app, try warehouse club stations, keep your tires inflated, and slow down on the highway.  
Two: get ahead of winter. Setting your thermostat back seven to ten degrees for eight hours a day can save up to ten percent a year on heating, according to the Department of Energy.  
Three: attack variable-rate debt first. Credit card rates move with the Fed, so pay those down before rates climb further.  
Four: if you're buying a home, shop at least three lenders. Even a small rate difference saves thousands.  
Five: make your cash work. Higher rates also mean high-yield savings accounts pay more. Your emergency fund should be earning something.  

## END

A war thousands of miles away has quietly reached into almost every American wallet.  
You can't control oil prices. But you can control how much of the damage reaches you.  
Subscribe to The Money Map, and we will keep tracking what this means for your money.
